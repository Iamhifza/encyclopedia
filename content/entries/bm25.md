## Simple Explanation

Score a document by which of the query's words it contains, weighting rare words
more heavily than common ones, discounting repeated occurrences, and correcting
for document length so long documents do not win by sheer size.

That is the whole idea, it was settled in the 1990s, and it is still the default
in every serious search system.

## Technical Definition

A probabilistic ranking function scoring a document $d$ against a query $q$ by
summing, over query terms, an inverse-document-frequency weight multiplied by a
saturating function of term frequency, normalised by document length relative to
the collection average.

## Why Does It Exist?

TF-IDF weighted terms sensibly but had two defects: term frequency grew without
limit, so a document repeating a word fifty times scored absurdly high, and long
documents accumulated matches simply by being long. BM25 fixes both with two
tunable parameters.

## What Problem Does It Solve?

Ranking by lexical relevance in a way that survives real corpora, where documents
vary enormously in length and term frequency is a noisy signal.

## Formula

$$\text{score}(q,d) = \sum_{t \in q} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{f(t,d)\,(k_1+1)}{f(t,d) + k_1\left(1 - b + b\frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}$$

* $f(t,d)$ — how often term $t$ appears in document $d$.
* $\text{IDF}(t)$ — inverse document frequency; a term in every document carries
  almost no signal, a rare one carries a great deal.
* $|d|$, $\text{avgdl}$ — this document's length and the collection average.
* $k_1$ — **saturation**, typically 1.2–2.0. The tenth occurrence of a word adds
  far less than the second. This is the fix TF-IDF lacked.
* $b$ — **length normalisation**, typically 0.75. At $b=1$ length is fully
  corrected for; at $b=0$ not at all.

## How Does It Work?

```text
query: "kv cache fragmentation"

  "kv"            rare      → high IDF, contributes a lot
  "cache"         common    → moderate IDF
  "fragmentation" rare      → high IDF

document scores = Σ (IDF × saturated, length-normalised frequency)

saturation curve:
  contribution ▲  ▁▃▅▆▇▇▇▇▇  ← flattens; the 20th mention barely counts
               └────────────▶ occurrences
```

## Mental Model

A librarian who has memorised which words are unusual. Mentioning "fragmentation"
tells them a great deal; mentioning "the" tells them nothing.

## Example

BM25's persistence is the interesting fact about it. Neural retrieval papers are
still expected to benchmark against it, and it still sometimes wins —
particularly out of domain, where a dense encoder trained elsewhere degrades and
BM25, which was never trained at all, does not. Its failure is equally
predictable: no shared vocabulary, no match. "My card got declined" finds nothing
in a document titled "payment authorisation failures".

## Real-World Usage

The default ranking function in Lucene, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Vespa and most
search infrastructure — usually one configuration flag away. In RAG stacks it is
the lexical half of hybrid retrieval, and omitting it is one of the most common
causes of a pipeline that cannot find an error code.

## Common Confusions

* **BM25 is not "just keyword search"** — the ranking is the whole point;
  boolean matching would return the same set unordered.
* **BM25 vs TF-IDF** — same intuition, better mechanics. Saturation and length
  normalisation are what make it work on real collections.
* **Parameters are rarely the problem** — defaults are good. If BM25 is
  underperforming, look at analysis and tokenisation first.

## Why Should I Care?

It is the strongest evidence in this encyclopedia that older is not worse: a
ranking function from the 1990s remains a component of state-of-the-art retrieval
systems, and leaving it out is a common and avoidable mistake.
